# Pi 4B real event-loop measurements — 2026-09-30

## What was measured

A bounded, scheduled-arrival **execution-substrate probe**, not a complete trading engine: one producer on CPU3, one consumer/event-loop owner on CPU2, 128 iterations of identical unsigned dependency-chain synthetic work/event, raw vs normal vs fast flow. All backends are built with exceptions enabled under Clang 19.1.7, `-std=c++14 -O3 -DNDEBUG -pthread -march=native -Wall -Wextra -Werror`. GLib 2.84.4, libuv 1.50.0. No DPDK, sockets, NIC, real order book, strategy, exchange or OMS is included.

Backends: existing busy-poll `simple_executor`; existing gsource executor with its fixed 10-task budget and eventfd writes; benchmark-only libuv MPSC/uv_async adapter, budgets 10 and 64. The gsource vs libuv comparison is between these **specific adapters**, not a claim that one event-loop library is universally faster. The new simple baseline in this same matrix controls build/error-type/workload differences from the older no-exception snapshot.

Main matrix: capacity 4096, 4 backend/budget configurations × 3 lanes × 6 profiles × 3 repetitions = 216 cases. Supplemental capacities 256/1024/16384: fast lane × overload/burst/stall × 4 configurations × 3 repetitions = 108 cases. Total **324 cases / 9,474,048 validated events**. Idle1k is capped at 1024 events/run; other cases have 32768 events. No scheduled events are dropped; planned timestamps remain unchanged if submission is delayed. Every case validates checksums, exactly one successful callback/event, timestamp ordering and completion cleanup fence.

Raw CSV `pi4b-20260930.csv` combines the original main/supplement files. `pi4b-20260930-summary.md` reports medians and per-run p99 ranges; these are not pooled population quantiles. Metadata/source digests: `pi4b-20260930-env.txt`.

## Low offered load: consumer CPU versus wake latency

Fast lane, capacity 4096; medians of three per-run metrics:

| Profile | Backend / budget | p50 us | p99 us | Consumer CPU, one core |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| idle1k | simple / busy-poll | 2.357 | 14.494 | 99.99% |
| idle1k | gsource / 10 | 11.642 | 762.888 | 1.93% |
| idle1k | libuv / 10 | 10.803 | 1324.601 | 1.62% |
| idle1k | libuv / 64 | 10.619 | 109.027 | 1.64% |
| steady100k | simple / busy-poll | 2.230 | 2.854 | 99.99% |
| steady100k | gsource / 10 | 9.379 | 11.848 | 74.91% |
| steady100k | libuv / 10 | 9.060 | 12.234 | 68.16% |
| steady100k | libuv / 64 | 9.107 | 12.183 | 68.11% |

Event loops substantially reduce **consumer** CPU at low traffic, but typical wake-to-work latency is higher than keeping a core continuously polling. The producer busy-waits in every variant, so these numbers are not total-process CPU or energy savings.

Idle1k tails are noisy: gsource fast per-run p99 90/763/1074us; libuv10 1325/98/1619us; libuv64 98/136/109us. In this shared-host run, one libuv10 steady100k fast repetition also reached p99 **3400.853us**, despite the median being 12.234us. These outliers must be retained, not hidden behind p50 or a median. We did not profile their origin or isolate CPUs/control power states, so attribution to kernel scheduling, sleep states, loop code or unrelated load is unconfirmed. Batch 64 vs 10 should have little intrinsic meaning when only one event is waiting: observed idle-tail differences are not proof of a batching benefit.

## Higher offered load

Fast, capacity 4096, steady500k:

| Backend / budget | Completed/s | p50 us | p99 us | Consumer CPU |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| simple | 500003 | 1.762 | 18.245 | 99.94% |
| gsource / 10 | 500000 | 3.624 | 45.336 | 96.97% |
| libuv / 10 | 499997 | 2.724 | 42.742 | 96.84% |
| libuv / 64 | 499988 | 2.676 | 30.544 | 96.93% |

All fast configurations keep up with this finite workload. Consumer CPU is now nearly saturated: event-driven does not mean cheap at all rates. At 100k/s much of the latency is wake cost; at 500k/s batching/continuous work reduces typical per-event latency. This is not a promise that increasing real traffic always improves latency.

Normal at steady500k does not keep up: completed rates about 0.372M/s (gsource10), 0.404M/s (uv10), 0.445M/s (uv64), 0.440M/s (simple). Corresponding E2E p99 about 22.52/15.59/8.65/9.57ms. Full ownership/cancellation machinery is not free, although exact cost attribution still needs profiling.

## Batch budget amortization under overload and bursts

Fast, capacity 4096, 2M planned arrivals/s:

| Backend / budget | Completed/s | E2E p99 ms |
|---|---:|---:|
| simple | 677170 | 31.775 |
| gsource / 10 | 516197 | 46.712 |
| libuv / 10 | 613550 | 36.660 |
| libuv / 64 | 699626 | 30.200 |

For these adapters, increasing libuv callback budget 10→64 improves observed overload throughput about **14%**, and reduces burst8192 E2E p99 13.53→11.98ms. Under bursts the uv64 consumer is active about 62.8% versus 71.1% for uv10. This is evidence for amortizing loop iterations in this synthetic load; **timer/other-IO fairness is not measured**, so 64 is a starting experiment, not a universally safe default. Uv64 marginally exceeds the simple baseline's overload rate in these runs; there is no statistical basis here for a general claim that libuv beats polling.

## Capacity still absorbs, rather than removes, waits

Fast libuv64, 500k/s with one 5ms consumer pause:

| Capacity | E2E p99 ms | Submit-lag p99 | Peak outstanding |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 256 | 4.842 | 4.327ms | 258 |
| 1024 | 4.873 | 2.822ms | 1026 |
| 4096 | 4.871 | 8.666us | 2536 |
| 16384 | 4.886 | 0.759us | 2536 |

Capacity 4096 covers the roughly 2500 arrivals during this pause. Neither 4096 nor 16384 erases the approximately 5ms interruption; the bigger queue mainly reduces producer lag. Gsource and libuv10 show the same broad pattern, with more near-capacity service pressure. Multi-producer traffic and different work costs may require a different budget.

## Verification / limits

- Release smoke: 72 small matrix cases, all validations passed.
- UBSan: 72 small matrix cases with fatal recovery disabled, passed.
- libuv self-test in Release and UBSan: queue capacities 1/2 × callback budgets 1/10/64, nested owner submission with full queues, 1000 external tasks/configuration, notification coalescing/rearm and synchronized handle closure, passed.
- Existing gsource contract test built with UBSan and passed.
- ASan/TSan not run in this round; ASan previously failed runtime startup on this host. Existing full repository suite not rerun; only standalone benchmark files/docs were added, no production executor change.
- No true IO/timer fairness, cancellation-under-load, multi-producer, long soak, realtime scheduling, power-state control or real trading-chain tests.

## Conclusion

A libuv-driven ff **fast** execution layer looks viable for a quantitative-trading prototype with a realistic, non-hard-realtime latency budget. Start experimentally with capacity 4096 / callback budget 64, then measure actual strategy and network IO. Existing gsource is also usable, but its fixed budget and notification cost produce a lower overload ceiling in this probe. Neither event-loop choice establishes strict microsecond tail guarantees: real sleeping wakeups and shared-host outliers need their own design/measurement work.
